## CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION

### Section 1.1 Background
### [ADD] — Insert after the first paragraph ("…using a consumer chat application as a marker is an existing practice.")

**[NEW paragraph — Consumer access as a distinct evidence need]:**

> The distinction between API access and consumer access is not merely logistical; it defines a different evidence need. Studies using API access answer what large language models *can* do under controlled conditions—fixed model versions, specified sampling parameters, reproducible pipelines. Studies using consumer access answer what teachers *will get* under the conditions they actually face—undocumented model changes, default sampling, no version control. The two evidence bases are complementary but not interchangeable: a finding established under API conditions does not transfer automatically to consumer conditions, and vice versa. The present study treats consumer access as a distinct research condition whose scoring properties require their own empirical investigation.

### [ADD] — Insert after the paragraph on L2 learners ("…has not, to the author's knowledge, been addressed in the published literature reviewed in Chapter 2.")

**[NEW paragraph — Kurdistan context as a contribution]:**

> The study's setting contributes to geographic diversity in AI scoring research. Virtually all empirical studies of LLM scoring of second-language writing reviewed in Chapter 2 use East Asian populations (Chinese, Japanese, Korean) or, less frequently, Latin American or European learners. Kurdish–Arabic–English trilingual adolescents are absent from this literature. The Kurdistan Region of Iraq, where private English-medium schooling has expanded significantly (Hassun, 2022), represents a growing population of multilingual learners sitting first-language examinations under conditions that have not been studied. The study does not claim generalisability from one school to the region, but it provides the first empirical evidence on AI scoring of this population's writing.

> If AI scoring introduces construct-irrelevant variance for multilingual populations sitting first-language examinations, it compounds an existing equity concern in international school assessment. Multilingual learners in English-medium international schools are routinely assessed against first-language expectations (Carder, 2007), and the appropriateness of this practice is itself debated (Gallagher, 2008; Sears, 2015). An AI scoring tool that amplifies construct-irrelevant penalisation of multilingual language features would deepen, rather than mitigate, this structural disadvantage.

## CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

### Section 2.2 Accessibility, L2 writers and the first-language construct
### [ADD] — Insert after the Carder (2007) reference, before the L2 writing literature paragraph

**[NEW paragraph — International school assessment equity]:**

> The tension between first-language assessment expectations and multilingual learner populations extends beyond individual classrooms. Gallagher (2008) examined the challenges of assessing English language learners within international school curricula designed for native speakers, arguing that assessment frameworks developed for monolingual populations may systematically disadvantage multilingual students. Sears (2015) found that international school teachers held varied and sometimes contradictory beliefs about how language proficiency should be weighed in content assessment. These findings suggest that the construct validity of first-language English examinations for multilingual learners is an unresolved question in international education. An AI scoring tool that inherits or amplifies the construct-irrelevant features of its training data could deepen this inequity without the corrective judgement that an experienced human marker might apply.

### [ADD] — New paragraph after "Passes and timing" — Model-change decision rule

**[NEW — Preregistered model-change decision rule]:**

> If the flagship model available in the model picker changes between any two passes for either tool, the following preregistered decision rule applies. The affected tool's data are analysed separately by model version as a primary analysis: pass-to-pass consistency (RQ1) is computed only within the passes that share the same model version, and agreement with human raters (RQ2) is reported separately for each model version. A combined-pass analysis pooling across model versions is reported as supplementary, with the model change entered as a covariate. This rule ensures that pass-to-pass consistency estimates are not confounded by model differences.

### Section 3.6 Pilot study
### [ADD] — New paragraph after desk pilot, before live pilot — Prompt-variation check

**[NEW — Prompt-variation check]:**

> Prompt-variation check. During the desk pilot, 5–10 practice scripts will be scored under two or three minor prompt variants in addition to the primary rubric-embedded framing. Variants will test specific prompt features that prior research has shown to affect agreement (Sawaki et al., 2025; Sclar et al., 2024): for example, (a) the primary framing with the reading key omitted, (b) the primary framing with the output-format line omitted, or (c) the primary framing with the order of rubric and reading key reversed. Each variant is scored once per script per tool. The results are compared descriptively (mean scores and within-script differences) against the primary framing to provide evidence that the chosen prompt is not producing anomalous results relative to reasonable alternatives. The prompt-variation check is reported in the thesis as a design-validation exercise; it does not constitute a formal prompt-optimisation study, and the primary framing remains unchanged regardless of the results. This check pre-empts the concern, raised by Sawaki et al. (2025), that AI scoring agreement is sensitive to prompt design: it provides evidence that the study's findings are not artefacts of a single prompt formulation.

### [REVISED] — Focused content analysis coding procedure

**Old:**
> Coding will be conducted by the candidate. The supervisor will review the coding of three selected scripts (comprising up to six AI rationales) and discuss discrepancies with the candidate; this serves as a credibility check, not comprehensive independent validation.

**New:**
> Coding will be conducted independently by both the candidate and the second human rater using the same coding scheme. Both coders will complete the full evidence-checking procedure for all 10 selected scripts (up to 20 rationale texts) before comparing their coding. Inter-coder agreement will be reported as percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa on the thematic codes. Disagreements will be discussed and resolved where possible; unresolved cases will be documented and reported. The supervisor will review the final coding of three selected scripts as an additional credibility check. This dual-coding procedure strengthens the qualitative strand's trustworthiness and addresses a limitation common in prior research on AI scoring rationales, where coding was conducted by a single researcher.

### [REVISED] — Writing mark as secondary — add to analysis hierarchy

**[ADD] — Insert after "Units, missing data and uncertainty" paragraph:**

> Primary and secondary criteria. The reading mark (/10) is the primary criterion for all analyses in RQ1 and RQ2. Its 11-point scale (0–10) provides sufficient variance for reliable estimation of agreement statistics. The writing mark (/5) is analysed as a secondary criterion: its 6-point scale (0–5) compresses variance and limits the precision of agreement statistics. All analyses are reported for both criteria, but interpretive weight is placed on the reading mark. The total mark (/15) is reported as a supplementary measure. This hierarchy reflects a design constraint inherent in the IGCSE marking scheme, not an analytical choice.

**New (RQ1 row):**

### [ADD] — New sub-section after synthesis, before Section 3.8

**[NEW — Section 3.7.x: Exploratory teacher-judgement exercise]:**

> Teacher-judgement exercise (exploratory). After the main analysis is complete, a brief exploratory exercise will examine whether the study's correction-gap evidence is interpretable and actionable for practising teachers. Five to ten IGCSE-experienced colleagues (not the study's two raters) will be shown the correction-gap profile—including AI and human scores, the direction and magnitude of the gap, and a summary of the AI's scoring rationale—for three to five selected scripts spanning different discrepancy levels. Each colleague will be asked: (a) whether the evidence would change their own marking decision; (b) which piece of evidence (consistency, agreement or rationale) they found most informative; and (c) whether they would trust the AI tool as a second marker for this task based on the evidence presented. Responses will be collected through a short structured questionnaire and reported descriptively. This exercise does not validate the discrepancy-review workflow operationally; it provides preliminary evidence on whether the study's outputs are useful to the teachers for whom they are intended, and it identifies directions for future research on teacher uptake. Ethics approval for this exercise will be sought as part of the main ethics application.

### Section 3.8 Ethics and data protection
### [REVISED] — Insider research paragraph

**Old:**
> Insider research. Because the candidate is the students' teacher, the researcher and a rater, the consent process will make clear that study participation has no bearing on grades. The typing, blinding and locking procedures address the rating role.

**New:**
> Insider research. The candidate occupies three roles: the students' teacher, the principal researcher and Rater 1. This triple role is the study's most significant ethical and methodological tension, and is mitigated at multiple levels. First, the consent process will make clear that study participation has no bearing on grades, and consent collection will not be conducted by the candidate in his teaching role. Second, all scripts are anonymised, typed verbatim and stripped of identifying content by an external anonymiser before the candidate sees any script. Third, scripts are marked in randomised order, blind to student identity and to all AI scores, with marks locked before AI scoring begins. Fourth, formal marking begins no earlier than four weeks after the last writing session, increasing temporal distance between classroom interaction and assessment. Fifth, the second human rater provides an independent, fully blind benchmark: where the candidate's marks diverge systematically from the second rater's, this is detectable in the H1–H2 agreement statistics. Sixth, the candidate will not conduct the re-checking of typed scripts against originals; this is the anonymiser's responsibility. Despite these mitigations, the candidate's familiarity with his students' writing abilities cannot be fully eliminated. This is acknowledged as a limitation; the second rater's marks serve as the primary check against unconscious bias.
